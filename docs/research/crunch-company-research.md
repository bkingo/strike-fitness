# Crunch Fitness Company Research

## Purpose

This document consolidates the existing company deep dive on the real company **Crunch Fitness**. It is intended as a durable research reference for later work on the fictional Strike Fitness business. It does not define a strategy, positioning, audience, offer, or brand identity for Strike Fitness.

The source research was assembled from Crunch Fitness announcements and press releases, the 2025 Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD), Franchise Times, William Blair research on the high-value, low-price fitness segment, Fitt Insider, and other company announcements published in 2025–2026. Figures are approximate unless otherwise noted.

## Evidence Classification

- **Verified fact:** Presented as supported by the source set used in the original research.
- **Reasonable inference:** A conclusion that follows from reported facts but was not itself directly established by a cited source.
- **Strategic interpretation:** An analytical judgment about the significance of the evidence.
- **Unknown / limited evidence:** A claim that is estimated, insufficiently sourced, forward-looking, or requires additional validation.

Because the original research retained source categories rather than claim-level links, “verified” in this document means supported by that research set, not independently re-audited against primary documents during this conversion.

## Executive Summary

### Verified facts

- Crunch Fitness is a franchise-led gym company founded in 1989 in Greenwich Village, New York City.
- Its brand is built around the “No Judgments®” positioning and a mix of conventional gym access, group fitness, personal training, and recovery amenities.
- The company reported more than 600 clubs and approximately 3.5 million members across 41 U.S. states, Washington, D.C., and multiple international markets.
- The research reported approximately $1.2 billion in 2024 systemwide sales and a goal of exceeding $2 billion in 2026.
- Leonard Green & Partners acquired a majority stake in 2025 at a reported valuation above $1.5 billion.
- Crunch operates primarily through franchises, with a smaller corporate-owned Signature portfolio.

### Reasonable inferences

- Franchising has enabled Crunch to expand faster and with less corporate capital than a predominantly company-owned model would require.
- The membership tier structure is designed to attract price-sensitive members at the entry level and increase revenue through classes, multi-club access, guest privileges, and recovery amenities.
- Group fitness, personal training, and recovery services give Crunch more engagement and upsell mechanisms than equipment-only value gyms.

### Strategic interpretations

- Crunch competes in a “value-plus” position: more programming and amenities than a basic low-price gym, while remaining materially less expensive than boutique and premium fitness options.
- Its central growth challenge is to preserve a consistent member experience while opening clubs at franchise scale.
- Its opportunity is not simply to underprice Planet Fitness. It is to offer more perceived value per dollar to members who want a fuller fitness experience.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- Private-company financial statements, franchisor revenue, EBITDA, debt, member churn, cohort retention, and club-level margins were not available in the source research.
- The $2 billion systemwide-sales figure is a target, not an established result.
- International unit economics and the economics of newer formats such as Crunch Select were not established.

## Company Background and Evolution

### Verified facts

- **Founded:** 1989 in a Greenwich Village basement studio.
- **Brand idea:** “Serious exercise made fun,” supported by the “No Judgments®” brand platform.
- **2009:** Crunch entered bankruptcy during the Great Recession.
- **2010:** The company began its franchise expansion, with the first franchise reported in Norwalk, Connecticut.
- **2019:** TPG Growth invested in Crunch.
- **2025:** Leonard Green & Partners acquired a majority stake at a reported valuation above $1.5 billion.
- **2026:** Chequan Lewis became CEO after Jim Rowley’s tenure, during which the system reportedly grew from 36 clubs to more than 600.

### Reasonable inference

The post-bankruptcy move toward franchising was the company’s defining structural change. It shifted much of the capital and execution burden of new locations to franchisees while preserving royalty and brand-fund income for the franchisor.

### Strategic interpretation

Crunch’s history suggests that its present scale is less the result of a single fitness product innovation than of combining a distinctive brand with a repeatable franchise model.

## Footprint and Operating Formats

### Verified facts

- The reported U.S. footprint spans 41 states and Washington, D.C.
- Reported international markets include Australia, Canada, Costa Rica, India, Mexico, New Zealand, Portugal, Puerto Rico, and Spain.
- The source research identified three principal formats:
  - **Crunch Fitness:** The core high-value, low-price franchise format.
  - **Crunch Select:** A newer premium franchise concept with boutique-inspired studios, digital booking, and more than 60 hours of classes per week.
  - **Crunch Signature:** A portfolio of 28 corporate-owned urban clubs with services such as upgraded locker rooms, saunas, steam rooms, and towel service.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The source research did not provide a market-by-market club list or reconcile exact domestic, international, franchised, and corporate totals.
- The long-term role, rollout pace, and unit economics of Crunch Select remain unproven in the available evidence.

## Business Model

### Verified facts

Crunch’s reported revenue model includes:

- Membership dues across Base, Peak, and Peak Results tiers.
- Franchise royalties equal to 5% of gross sales.
- A 2% brand-fund contribution from franchisees.
- Personal training, including one-to-one and small-group offerings such as HIITZone.
- Ancillary services such as tanning, HydroMassage, red-light therapy, and wellness or recovery offerings.
- Crunch+, a live and on-demand digital workout service offered through eligible memberships and as a standalone subscription.

Typical membership pricing in the source research was:

- **Base:** Approximately $9.99–$19.99 per month for single-club access and core equipment.
- **Peak:** Approximately $22–$27 per month, adding group classes and multi-club access.
- **Peak Results:** Approximately $25–$35 per month, adding advanced classes, guest privileges, and recovery amenities.
- **Signature:** Approximately $85–$130 per month at premium urban clubs.

Pricing and benefits vary by club and market.

### Reasonable inferences

- The Base tier serves as an acquisition offer for price-sensitive consumers.
- Peak and Peak Results are intended to increase average revenue per member by packaging services with higher perceived value.
- Crunch+ can support retention and extend the brand beyond visits to a physical club, although the source research did not quantify either effect.

### Strategic interpretation

The tier ladder is central to Crunch’s economics. Entry pricing broadens the acquisition funnel, while classes, guests, training, and recovery provide reasons to upgrade. This gives Crunch a broader monetization architecture than a basic access-only gym.

## Franchise Economics

### Verified facts

The 2025 FDD figures retained in the source research were:

- **Estimated initial investment:** Approximately $928,000–$3.74 million.
- **Initial franchise fee:** $35,000.
- **Royalty:** 5% of gross sales.
- **Brand fund:** 2%.
- **Average gross sales for mature clubs:** Approximately $2.77 million.
- **Typical target club size:** Approximately 25,000–30,000 square feet.
- **Multi-unit ownership:** Approximately 40% of U.S. franchisees reportedly operate three or more clubs.

### Reasonable inferences

- Multi-unit ownership can create local operating leverage, management depth, and denser regional marketing.
- Second-generation gym conversions may reduce construction work and shorten opening timelines relative to building from an empty shell.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The original research estimated a three-to-four-year payback period, but it did not retain enough evidence to verify that estimate. It should not be treated as an established franchise outcome.
- Average gross sales do not establish franchisee profitability. Rent, labor, equipment financing, presale performance, member mix, personal-training economics, and local competition can materially change returns.
- The reported investment range does not show where most new clubs fall within that range.

## Customer Value Proposition and Brand

### Verified facts

- Crunch combines low entry pricing with extensive free weights, group fitness, personal training, functional training, and selected recovery amenities.
- The company reports more than 230 proprietary classes.
- Its brand voice is inclusive, energetic, and deliberately less formal than traditional health clubs.
- Signature and Select formats address customers seeking a more elevated experience than the core format.

### Reasonable inferences

The likely primary customer is a value-conscious consumer who wants more programming and amenities than a basic gym provides but does not want boutique-studio pricing. Other plausible audiences include beginners seeking an inclusive environment and urban professionals attracted to premium formats.

### Strategic interpretation

Crunch’s differentiation is the combination of affordability and participation. Classes, training, and recovery can make the club feel like an active fitness experience rather than only a room of equipment.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The research did not include customer segmentation studies, brand tracking, demographic profiles, willingness-to-pay research, or direct member interviews.
- The target-customer descriptions above are analytical profiles, not verified company personas.

## Product and Format Development

### Crunch 3.0

#### Verified facts

The Crunch 3.0 redesign was described as a modular template for new and existing clubs. Reported components include:

- Redesigned entrances and lobbies.
- Modernized group-fitness studios with updated lighting, mirrors, and digital timers.
- Expanded HIITZone and turf-based functional training.
- Relax & Recover® areas featuring offerings such as Hyperice equipment, red-light therapy, and infrared saunas.
- Optional “Hot Stuff” heated studios for yoga, Pilates, barre, and mobility.
- Enhanced personal-training zones.

The research reported 125 openings using the Crunch 3.0 approach and plans for more than 100 additional remodels by 2027.

#### Strategic interpretation

Crunch 3.0 appears designed to improve the in-club experience, strengthen premium-tier value, and keep older locations competitive without abandoning the entry-price proposition.

### Crunch Select

#### Verified facts

- Crunch Select is a premium franchise format.
- It emphasizes boutique-inspired studios, digital-first booking, refined hospitality, and more than 60 hours of classes per week.
- The first reported location was in Long Beach, New York, in 2026.

#### Unknowns or limited evidence

The research did not establish member demand, pricing durability, build cost, margins, or whether Select can scale without confusing the distinction between core Crunch and Signature.

### Crunch Signature

#### Verified facts

- The source research reported 28 corporate-owned Signature locations concentrated in the San Francisco, Los Angeles, Miami, and New York City metropolitan areas.
- Amenities include full-service locker rooms, saunas, steam rooms, and towel service.

#### Reasonable inference

Signature functions as both a premium business line and a brand halo in major urban markets.

### Crunch+

#### Verified facts

- Crunch+ offers live and on-demand workouts across devices.
- It is bundled with eligible all-club memberships and is also available as a standalone subscription.

#### Unknowns or limited evidence

No subscriber count, engagement rate, retention impact, acquisition cost, or revenue contribution was available.

## Growth Strategy

### Verified facts

The source research described the following growth activities:

- A goal of reaching 1,000 clubs.
- A pace of more than two openings per week and more than 100 new gyms per year.
- Use of multi-unit franchisees to build regional density.
- Conversion of existing gym sites, including former 24 Hour Fitness locations in Florida.
- Use of vacant big-box retail and anchor spaces.
- International master-franchise agreements, including a reported 75-location development agreement in India.
- Crunch 3.0 remodels and the introduction of Crunch Select.
- A reported opening timeline of six to nine months, with a six-month target for some second-generation conversions.

### Reasonable inferences

- Existing gym sites can provide suitable footprints, power, plumbing, locker rooms, and market familiarity, potentially improving speed to market.
- Big-box retail vacancies can match Crunch’s preferred square footage and may give landlords an experiential tenant that generates recurring traffic.
- Concentrating clubs under experienced multi-unit operators may accelerate growth and improve regional execution.

### Strategic interpretations

- Growth is funded by private-equity ownership and executed largely by franchisees.
- The model can compound quickly when established franchisees reinvest in additional units.
- The main tension is speed versus consistency: rapid openings create scale, but weak execution at individual franchises can damage the national brand.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The 1,000-club objective and opening targets are forward-looking.
- The source research did not establish signed-pipeline conversion rates, site-level opening delays, closure rates, or new-club ramp curves.
- Reported regional membership growth figures—88% in the Lower Midwest and 35% in the Mid-Atlantic in 2025—need validation and context, including whether they reflect new units, same-club growth, or both.

## Leadership and Ownership

### Verified facts

- **Chequan Lewis:** Reported CEO beginning in 2026; previously associated with brand and marketing leadership, including work at Popeyes.
- **Jim Rowley:** CEO from 2009 to 2026; credited in the source research with overseeing growth from 36 to more than 600 clubs.
- **Molly Rowley:** Reported COO with responsibility related to operations and Crunch 3.0 execution.
- **Leonard Green & Partners:** Majority owner following the 2025 transaction.
- **TPG Growth:** Investor during the 2019–2025 growth period.
- The TPG period was reported to include 176% membership growth and 275 new clubs.

### Strategic interpretation

The transition to a CEO with a marketing background may indicate greater emphasis on brand, member experience, retention, and franchisee economics after a period dominated by unit expansion. This is an interpretation, not a confirmed change in corporate priorities.

## Major Franchisee: CR Fitness Holdings

### Verified facts

The original research identified CR Fitness Holdings as Crunch’s largest franchisee and reported:

- Approximately 90 clubs across Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Texas, and Tennessee.
- Acquisition of nine former 24 Hour Fitness locations in 2025.
- Development rights for more than 200 additional units.
- A plan to open more than 100 clubs over five years.
- Backing associated with North Castle Partners; Sixth Street was also identified in the broader franchisee private-equity context.

### Reasonable inference

CR Fitness Holdings demonstrates how a well-capitalized multi-unit operator can become a regional growth engine through both new development and conversion of existing gyms.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

The claim that CR Fitness operates the system’s highest-volume units was included in the original research but was not supported by retained unit-level data.

## Scale and Engagement Indicators

### Verified facts

The source research reported:

- More than 600 clubs worldwide.
- Approximately 3.5 million members.
- Approximately $1.2 billion in 2024 systemwide sales.
- A target of more than $2 billion in 2026 systemwide sales.
- 91 clubs opened in 2025.
- Approximately 94% of clubs franchised.
- 847,384 group-fitness classes delivered in 2025.
- 11.3 million class attendees in 2025, up 36% year over year.
- More than 10,000 instructors.
- 78% year-over-year growth in mind-body classes.
- Approximately 6,000 members per club on a simple systemwide average.

### Reasonable inferences

- Growth in class attendance is consistent with demand for Crunch’s programming-led value proposition.
- High class participation can support retention and paid-tier upgrades, but the available evidence does not establish causation.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The claim that members completing a CrunchONE Kickoff are “80% more likely to hit goals” lacks a retained methodology, sample definition, and outcome measure.
- Aggregate members per club is only a rough ratio. It does not account for club age, format, market, capacity, or inactive memberships.
- The source research did not provide same-store sales, visits per member, tier mix, personal-training penetration, net member additions, or cancellation rates.

## Competitive Landscape

### Verified facts

The original research reported the following approximate scale indicators:

- **Planet Fitness:** Approximately 2,700 clubs and 21 million members; about 6% growth in 2024.
- **Crunch Fitness:** Approximately 600 clubs and 3.5 million members; about 17% growth.
- **EōS Fitness:** Approximately 127 clubs and 1.5 million members; about 27% growth.
- **VASA Fitness:** Approximately 65 clubs; about 11% growth.
- **Chuze Fitness:** Approximately 57 clubs; about 10% growth.

These figures represent approximate company scale and reported growth, not a complete or consistently defined market-share dataset.

### Reasonable inferences

- Planet Fitness has a substantial advantage in scale and brand ubiquity.
- EōS, VASA, and Chuze compete with variants of a similar high-value, amenity-rich proposition in their strongest regions.
- Boutique studios compete for specialized workout occasions, while digital services compete for members’ time and attention.

### Strategic interpretations

- Against Planet Fitness, Crunch’s clearest differentiation is more extensive classes, free weights, personal training, and recovery amenities.
- Against boutique concepts, Crunch can offer broader gym access at a much lower monthly price, though with less specialization.
- Crunch has meaningful expansion whitespace, but that opportunity may narrow as competing value-gym concepts add locations and amenities.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The source set did not provide a common-period, independently normalized comparison of openings, closures, revenue, same-store performance, or member growth.
- Calling Crunch the “fastest-growing major high-value, low-price chain” depends on how “major” and “growth” are defined; EōS had a higher percentage growth figure in the same research.

## Strategic Risks

### Verified or observable risk factors

- **Franchise quality variation:** A highly franchised system can produce inconsistent service, cleanliness, sales practices, and facility quality.
- **Billing and cancellation friction:** Complaints or difficult cancellation processes can create reputational and regulatory exposure.
- **Peak-hour crowding:** High membership density can reduce the experience for frequent users.
- **Aggressive personal-training sales:** High-pressure selling can undermine trust.
- **Lease and real-estate costs:** Large-format gyms are exposed to rent, build-out, and occupancy risk.
- **Competitive expansion:** Planet Fitness has far greater scale, while EōS, VASA, and other regional chains offer overlapping value propositions.
- **Private-equity time horizons:** Ownership return requirements can increase pressure for rapid growth or margin improvement.

### Reasonable inferences

- Standardized operations, remodels, franchise support, and better member-feedback systems can reduce—but not eliminate—franchise inconsistency.
- Low entry pricing may provide some resilience during economic downturns, although members can still treat gym dues as discretionary spending.
- Second-generation conversions may mitigate some real-estate and build-out risk.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

- The original research included an estimated $27 million in annual value associated with cancellations, but it did not retain the calculation or source methodology. It should be treated as unverified.
- The research linked recovery and wellness offerings to the rise of GLP-1 medications. The business impact of GLP-1 adoption on gym acquisition, retention, and service demand remains uncertain.
- References to a retention-focused “3Rs” program—Revenue, Retention, and Referral—and unified feedback systems require primary-source confirmation.
- Mention of FTC “Click-to-Cancel” compliance described a relevant regulatory issue, not evidence of Crunch’s compliance status or a specific violation.

## Strategic Opportunities Identified in the Source Research

These are interpretations of potential opportunity, not established outcomes or recommendations for Strike Fitness.

### Near-term interpretations

- Complete planned Crunch 3.0 remodels to improve existing-club competitiveness.
- Test whether Crunch Select can capture higher willingness to pay without undermining the core brand.
- Use second-generation gym conversions to increase opening speed.
- Evaluate the India master-franchise agreement as a test of a repeatable international model.
- Explore artificial intelligence for member and employee experiences; the source research noted leadership interest but did not identify a defined program or measured outcome.

### Medium- and long-term interpretations

- Build toward the 1,000-club goal while protecting franchise quality.
- Develop Crunch+ as a digital layer supporting physical-club membership rather than treating it only as a standalone product.
- Use recovery, mobility, and wellness services to broaden the reasons members visit.
- Continue using experienced multi-unit franchisees to build regional density.

### Unknowns or limited evidence

The research did not include business cases, investment requirements, expected returns, consumer studies, or execution plans for these opportunities.

## Overall Strategic Assessment

### Strategic interpretation

Crunch Fitness appears to be pursuing a franchise-led “value-plus” growth strategy. Its offer combines a low entry price with classes, training, free weights, functional fitness, and recovery amenities that can support higher membership tiers. This places it between basic low-price gyms and higher-cost boutique or premium clubs.

The strategy’s strength is the interaction between brand, tiered monetization, and franchise scale. Its principal vulnerability is operational: the company must maintain a reliable experience across a rapidly expanding, predominantly franchised network.

### Important analytical caution

The original research compared a reported valuation above $1.5 billion with approximately $1.2 billion in systemwide sales. That ratio is roughly 1.25 times, but it is **not a conventional company revenue multiple**. Systemwide sales are the sales generated across franchised and corporate clubs, not the franchisor’s recognized revenue. Franchisor financial statements would be required for a valid revenue or EBITDA valuation multiple.

## Research Limitations

This research establishes a structured view of Crunch Fitness’s reported scale, model, brand, formats, growth activity, and competitive position. It is suitable for orientation and for identifying questions that later strategy work should investigate.

It does **not** establish audited financial performance, franchise profitability, causal links between amenities and retention, customer demand by segment, or the likely success of future openings and new formats. Crunch is privately held, and much of the available information comes from company announcements, franchise materials, trade reporting, and forward-looking targets. The original source set did not retain claim-level URLs, publication dates, or page references, so high-stakes decisions should be preceded by validation against primary documents.
